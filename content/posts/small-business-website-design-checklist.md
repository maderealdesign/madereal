## The homepage has a clear first job

It should help someone understand what the business does, whether it serves them and what to do next. A strong headline, short explanation, relevant image and obvious contact route usually communicate more than a screen full of slogans.

Check that your main service and actual location or coverage are clear. Put genuine work near the decision, not hidden behind an unfamiliar menu name. Explain the next step in everyday words.

## Give the important things room

Use a readable body font and a clear heading hierarchy. Keep lines of text comfortable to scan. If everything is bold, large and animated, nothing feels important.

Limit the core colours and give buttons a consistent purpose. Cards are useful for repeated projects or options, but every paragraph does not need a coloured box. An animation should help orientation or draw attention deliberately, not delay reading.

## Make the brand recognisable elsewhere

Your website, Google profile, social account and offline material should use a consistent business name, logo and contact details. That does not mean every item must look identical. It means a person moving between them should recognise the same business.

Use the right logo file rather than a blurry screenshot. Check colour contrast instead of assuming a brand colour will work for small text. A fashionable pale grey can become difficult to read on a phone outdoors.

## A proper mobile check

Test at a narrow phone width as well as your own device. Look for clipped headings, sideways scrolling, tiny buttons and fixed bars covering content. Open the menu, close it, expand FAQs and complete the form.

Try the keyboard on desktop. Links and buttons should have a visible focus indicator, and the focus order should make sense. Forms need visible labels, useful errors and a clear confirmation after a successful submission.

Images should have reserved dimensions so the page does not jump as they load. Compress them without making important detail unreadable. Respect reduced-motion preferences. Use a performance test to identify heavy resources, then check the real experience too; one laboratory score does not describe every visitor’s connection.

## Trust for professional services

An accountant, consultant or other professional service needs more than an attractive hero. Explain who provides the work, their genuine experience, the process and which clients the service is suitable for. Describe qualifications accurately rather than collecting impressive-looking badges.

Examples can be useful without revealing confidential details. Use permissioned case studies or clearly labelled illustrative scenarios, not an invented client result. Explain what happens after an enquiry and what information is appropriate to share. A first-contact form should not invite sensitive records unnecessarily.

“Arrange an initial conversation” may suit a complex advisory service better than “Buy now”. Match the next step to the actual buying process, and link to clear privacy information.

## Show real proof, with the right claim

A gallery demonstrates work. An attributed review records someone’s feedback. A measured case study can describe outcomes when the data and permission exist. These are different kinds of evidence; do not let a decorative star rating stand in for all three.
