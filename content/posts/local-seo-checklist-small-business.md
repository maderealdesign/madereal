## Explain the service and real coverage

Write down what you sell, who needs it and where you genuinely provide it. Build pages around distinct services and customer questions. Avoid replacing a clear explanation with a long string of town names.

A roofing business might need separate information about repairs and complete roof replacement because those customers have different questions. It does not automatically need fifty copies of the same page with different locations in the headline.

## Keep your Google Business Profile accurate

If your business is eligible, maintain accurate details, opening hours, categories and service information. Use your actual premises or appropriate service-area setup rather than inventing an address.

Google describes local results in terms of relevance, distance and prominence. You cannot pay Google for a better local ranking. Its [local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) is a better starting point than a promise of a secret shortcut.

## When does a location page deserve to exist?

A useful location page answers something specific: coverage boundaries, the work offered there, real local projects, access or visiting details, and relevant customer questions. Evidence matters more than repeating the place name.

If the service and process are identical across nearby towns and you have nothing distinct to add, a well-written coverage page may be better. Do not invent a local office, customer or completed job to make a thin page appear unique.

MadeReal’s [Colne](/locations/colne/) page can state that the business is based there. The [Skipton](/locations/skipton/) page describes serving that area from Colne and links to a genuine Skipton project; it does not pretend there is a MadeReal office in North Yorkshire.

## Write an article because someone needs the answer

Choose a question you actually hear before a sale. For a garden-room business, this might concern the information needed for an initial quote. For a food shop, it could be how to choose between products or plan an order.

Then follow a simple workflow:

1. Define the reader and the decision they are trying to make.
2. Answer directly near the start, in plain language.
3. Add the practical details, real examples and limitations that only the business can supply.
4. Use descriptive headings and original, permissioned images where they help.
5. Link to the relevant service or product and a sensible next step.
6. Check facts, publish with an honest date and review it when details change.
7. Assess relevant impressions, visits and enquiries over time, not just the number of posts published.

Do not churn out near-identical articles just to appear active. Do not turn an illustrative example into a claimed client result. [Google’s SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) emphasises useful content and understandable site structure; there is no guaranteed ranking formula.

## Check the technical basics

Important pages should load successfully, have a unique title and description, use one clear main heading and link to related pages. Check that the live site is not accidentally marked noindex. A sitemap should contain the final canonical pages, not broken or redirected URLs.

When replacing old URLs, use relevant direct redirects. Keep images lightweight, reserve their dimensions and test mobile navigation and forms. Do not block the resources search engines need to understand the page.

## Measure the right outcome

Search Console can show which queries and pages receive exposure and clicks. Your enquiries tell you whether visitors take the next step. Qualified enquiries and sales are further stages, not interchangeable numbers.

No website structure or special “AI file” guarantees ChatGPT recommendations. Publish accurate, accessible information with real evidence and clear attribution. That is work worth doing even when a particular platform does not send traffic.
