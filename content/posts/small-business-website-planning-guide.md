## What problem is the website solving?

Perhaps people cannot see your work, keep asking the same questions or cannot find your contact details. Perhaps your social page looks active but the website belongs to an older version of the business. Those are useful problems to solve.

A website may not be the immediate priority if you have not decided what you sell or cannot take on new work. Building one does not create demand automatically. Start with who you want to reach and the action you want them to take.

## A website and Facebook can do different jobs

Social media is useful for updates, conversations and sharing new work. A website gives you a place to organise lasting information: services, coverage, examples, menus, pricing and contact routes. One does not have to replace the other.

Use your social profile to bring people to a relevant page when they need more detail. Use the website to link to current social activity where it genuinely helps. Avoid making visitors log into another platform just to learn your opening hours.

## One landing page or several pages?

A focused landing page can work for one specific offer or campaign. For example, a page about a single garden-room product might explain the design, options and enquiry route in one place.

A multi-page site is more useful when different customers need different information. A roofer offering repairs, re-roofs and roofline work may need clear pages for each rather than squeezing everything into one long homepage. A food business may need a menu, visiting information and its story.

Choose based on the customer’s questions, not an arbitrary page count. A page with nothing distinct to say is not a useful addition just because a package allows it.

## Gather this before the build

- **Business details:** the correct name, contact number, email and real address or service area.
- **Services:** what you offer, who it is for, what is not included and which work you want more of.
- **Photos:** your own work, premises, team or products, with permission to use them.
- **Proof:** genuine reviews, qualifications and registrations, accurately described.
- **Practical information:** opening hours, quote process, booking options and common questions.
- **Brand assets:** logo files, preferred colours and examples you like or dislike.
- **Account control:** who owns the domain, existing hosting and Google profile.

Do not worry if you do not have everything. Mark gaps clearly. It is better to launch with honest, useful content than fill empty space with invented reviews or stock photos presented as completed jobs.

## Choose one main next step

Should visitors call for urgent help, ask for a quote, book a table or visit the shop? The primary button should say what happens. “Get a quote” is clearer than “Discover the possibilities” when the actual destination is a quote form.

Offer a sensible alternative too. Someone may not be able to call right now. A short enquiry form, email or WhatsApp link can help, provided someone will respond.

## Separate launch essentials from later ideas

Put accurate services, readable mobile layouts, working contact routes and essential legal information first. A complex animation or new customer portal can wait until its purpose is clear.
